Level 1

See it, say it, then write it

The order matters and it is not shortened: a drawing of a cut orange, then the name in words — "one quarter" — and only then the symbol. Most children who "can't do fractions" met the symbol first with nothing underneath it to attach it to. In this level the symbol appears only in the feedback after a correct answer — never in a question, and the rule check below fails the build if one leaks in.

Equal parts is the idea everyone skips

Two pieces are not halves unless they are the same size. Round 4 tests it deliberately, and two of its three oranges are cut into two unequal pieces — the exact distractor the idea needs. A child who never has this challenged will compare and simplify wrongly for years, and nothing downstream ever reveals why.

Change the shape, keep the fraction

One half turns up on a bun cut into a bar, on a round orange, and on a basket of six — three shapes, one idea, inside a single level. Children who only ever see circles bind the fraction to the picture and stall the moment a bar appears.

One whole, then a group

Half of one orange and half of six oranges are the same idea worn differently. Round 5 makes that jump on purpose — it is the bridge to "three quarters of 240 learners" three levels later, and without it that question reads as a different topic.

Objects they actually hold

Oranges, buns, chitenge, sweets. Switch the rig to plain worksheet shapes to see the version we are not building: the maths is identical and the picture stops being about anything. A worksheet circle is abstract; a cut orange is this morning.

Wrong answers get taught, not marked

Every distractor maps to one named confusion — naming by the number of pieces taken rather than the number made, or ignoring equal size. There is no generic "incorrect" anywhere in this level, and the check below counts the explanations to prove it.

Grade 4 reading level

Short sentences. No "numerator" and no "denominator" — "the top" and "the bottom" until the idea is solid; the formal words arrive in level 2. The longest sentence in the whole level is measured and printed below, because a reading-level rule nobody measures drifts within a week.
